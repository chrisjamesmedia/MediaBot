Dr. Rhonda Patrick@foundmyfitness · Nov 27

When I think about eating for brain health, I have two goals: supporting how my brain functions today and preserving that function as I get older. For me, that starts with consistently giving it the nutrients it needs.

Leafy greens bring a lot to the table. Magnesium is required for energy production and normal nerve signaling. Folate supports DNA synthesis and methylation, processes that help our cells function and regulate gene activity.

In one study, higher dietary magnesium intake was associated with larger gray matter and hippocampal volumes. Comparing intakes of about 550 mg with 350 mg per day, the researchers estimated a brain-volume difference equivalent to roughly one year of typical aging.

Then there are compounds like lutein in kale, beta-carotene in carrots, and anthocyanins in blueberries. Each plant contributes something different, which is why I care about variety.

The avocado has a job here, too. Along with making the smoothie creamy, its fat helps us absorb carotenoids from the vegetables. In a small study, adding avocado to a salad increased lutein absorption about fivefold.

There’s also so much about the chemistry of whole foods that we’re still discovering, including how their compounds interact with each other and with our gut microbes.

That’s part of why whole foods remain the foundation of my approach.

The consistency paradox or grandfather paradox occurs when the past is changed in any way, thus creating a contradiction.

The paradox suggests that a cause is eliminated by its own effect, thus preventing its own cause and essentially becoming reverse causation.

The grandfather paradox has been a trope of science fiction, appearing in Ray Bradbury's short story A Sound of Thunder, the classic movie Back to the Future and many other works. But the grandfather paradox isn't limited to the realm of fiction.

Philosophers and physicists began seriously thinking about the grandfather paradox when Albert Einstein's theories of special and general relativity suggested that time travel may be a theoretical possibility.

Dr. Rhonda Patrick@foundmyfitness · Nov 19

My thoughts on stevia, monk fruit, and allulose. These are three sugar alternatives I get asked about a lot.

Stevia - Some human research suggests modest benefits for blood glucose regulation and possibly blood pressure, although findings are mixed.

Monk fruit - One of my preferred alternatives to sugar. It has little effect on blood glucose, but long-term human safety data are limited. One caution: Many monk fruit products contain mostly erythritol, which I personally try to avoid because of emerging research on platelet activation.

Allulose - Small human studies suggest it may actually reduce post-meal glucose and insulin spikes. Promising, but we need more long-term research.

None of these were included in the recent study linking sugar substitutes to cognitive decline.

My personal approach? I generally favor stevia or pure monk fruit when I need a little sweetness, and I'm cautiously optimistic about allulose. But I try to minimize added sweeteners overall.

Dr. Rhonda Patrick@foundmyfitness

People consuming the most artificial sweeteners showed 63% faster cognitive decline than those consuming the least, roughly equivalent to 1.6 additional years of cognitive aging.

The middle-intake group also showed a 36% faster decline, equivalent to about 1.3 additional years of cognitive aging.

That’s from a Neurology study following 12,772 adults for about eight years. And when researchers looked separately by age, the association showed up in people under 60, but not those 60 and older.

Personally, I stay away from aspartame and sucralose, partly because of concerns about their potential effects on the gut microbiome. The platelet research has also made me more cautious about erythritol and xylitol. I do use stevia and monk fruit, but I look for versions without erythritol.

One practical detail that’s easy to miss - “monk fruit” on the front of a package doesn’t tell you everything inside. Check the ingredient list, because erythritol can be part of the blend.

Brigham Buhler@ferrisbuhler81 · Nov 28

INTERESTING STUFF Being cold makes your joints stiff primarily because low temperatures alter the physical properties of the synovial fluid that lubricates and cushions the joints. Synovial fluid acts much like motor oil; when the ambient temperature drops, this fluid becomes thicker and more viscous, creating increased internal resistance that makes moving the joint feel sluggish and rigid.
Cold weather also triggers a natural physiological defense mechanism designed to preserve core body temperature. Blood vessels in the extremities constrict to redirect warm blood toward vital internal organs, which reduces overall blood flow and warmth to the muscles and connective tissues surrounding the joints, leaving them less pliable and more tense.
Furthermore, dropping barometric pressure-which frequently accompanies cold weather fronts-causes the tissues around the joints to expand slightly. This subtle expansion places extra mechanical stress on the joint capsule and nerve endings, amplifying discomfort and making any existing stiffness or inflammation much more noticeable.
